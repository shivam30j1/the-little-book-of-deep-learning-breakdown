# 3.6 Training protocols

This chapter describes standard protocols around training, but the more interesting aspect in it is actually how deep models appear to _defy_ conventional wisdom. Training a deep model, as with any other machine learning model, is successful when it learns well from the training data _and_ can perform well on _new_, _unseen_ data. Training then, at a minimum, consists of two independent datasets: a training set used to train the model and a test set used to assess accuracy on unseen data. There is also usually a third set called the validation set which is used for tuning the hyperparameters of the model. As summary,

• Training set: Used to train the model, minimize the loss, optimize the parameters, and create the _mapping_.

• Validation set: Take the model trained on the training set, make predictions, assess accuracy. _Choose_ appropriate hyperparameters, train the model on the training set, validate again.

• Test set: Use _only_ to make predictions and assess final accuracy as an unbiased proxy for how the model will perform on new, unseen data.

The training process is usually composed of _epochs_. One epoch corresponds to going through all examples in the training set once. Each epoch may also be further divided to send data in batches (a noisier but more computationally efficient strategy). Typically, loss on training data decreases _as long as training continues_, while loss on validation data _decreases and then increases_ at a turning point. This corresponds to an _overfitting_ scenario where the model starts to learn _noise_ specific to the training data after this turning point, which hinders generalizability.

Here is the paradox: Large deep models appear to _defy_ this behavior in practice. Conventional wisdom suggests that the more complex a model, the more it can overfit. Large deep models should then massively overfit due to their capacity and their number of trainable parameters. However, their performance on the validation set usually _continues to improve_ as training progresses. This is _counterintuitive_. One line of reasoning could be that, at a sufficiently large scale of capacity, the inductive bias of the model (discussed in 1.3) becomes the primary driver of optimization when the performance is near perfect on the training set. 1.3 discusses how inductive bias is _foundational_ to deep learning (and broadly, machine learning). It also discusses that due to the nature of deep learning architectures, inductive bias can be _modularized_ and introduced at every level of a deep architecture. Then, at a sufficiently large scale, a deep learning model is _inductive bias galore!_

As accuracy approaches near perfection on the training set, a model with sufficiently appropriate inductive biases for the underlying data and at a sufficient scale may, _after_ a point, focus on learning _better_ from _existing_ signal compared to new signal. The double descent curve and the choice of the simplest fit is a deeper explanation, but beyond the scope of these notes [Belkin et al., 2018].

Stated plainly, _training continues on the training set_. Training loss reduces and accuracy on both the training and validation sets increases. At a certain point, training loss continues to decrease while validation loss starts to increase. From a classical perspective, this is the _entire picture_. However, modern deep models at a _sufficiently large scale_ have the validation loss start decreasing _again_ after a near-perfect or perfect accuracy on the training set, typically reaching levels _below_ the previous minimum.

One important design choice is that of the learning rate schedule, applying _different_ learning rates depending on the stage of training. A common strategy is to choose initially an _aggressive_ learning rate to avoid bad local minima early and then smaller learning rates later in the training process to allow for a smoother convergence to an appropriate local minimum (since it may bounce around at this point at a sufficiently large learning rate).

Training large deep models using gradient descent is iterative, converges _slowly_ towards a minimum, and may take _months_ and _millions_ of dollars or _more_. Interventions during training may be several, as informed by the evolution of the loss landscape (since the primary objective, at _any scale_ of learning, remains loss minimization).

## Fine-tuning
A model trained on a particular dataset can be _fine-tuned_ and used for prediction on a _separate_ but _related_ dataset/task. Such a procedure is similar to training a model from scratch on the new dataset except it starts with parameters pre-trained on the original dataset.

Fine-tuning may be optimal because examples for the new task are not enough but share similar structure and statistical properties with examples of the original task (e.g. a cat prediction model fine-tuned on pictures of dogs for a dog prediction task). It can also be to limit training costs for the new task. The key _is starting from the trained parameters of the original task_ since those represent all information value on the original dataset.

Most computer vision (CV) applications are structured as a fine-tuning of a model trained originally on the dataset ImageNet. This reveals an important point: since a model trained on ImageNet can be used for fine-tuning on a broad spectrum of computer vision tasks, images _in general, regardless of genre_, share _similar_ statistical properties. This may be a strong claim but seems a logical extension of the fact. Fine-tuning is also one way to purpose LLMs as assistants.
