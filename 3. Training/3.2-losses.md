# 3.2 Autoregressive Models

There’s more detail to what’s discussed in this section later in the book. An autoregressive setup is the *bedrock* of modern generative AI. These models are useful for handling *discrete* sequences, whether in natural language processing (e.g. documents/sentences) or computer vision (e.g. images). The key is that the sequence, regardless of length, is made up of separate, individual components.

The autoregression uses the *chain rule of probability* which, like most useful concepts discussed in the book, is simple but powerful. Given a set of discrete events, their probability of co-occurrence is the product of the conditional probabilities of each token given all previous tokens. Specifically,

$$
P(X_1, ..., X_T) = P(X_1) \times P(X_2 \mid X_1) \times P(X_3 \mid X_1, X_2) \times ... \times P(X_T \mid X_1, ..., X_{T-1})
$$

Or

$$
P(X_1, ..., X_T) = P(X_1) \times \prod_{t=2}^{T} P(X_t \mid X_1, ..., X_{t-1})
$$

It then follows that iterative sampling, *one at a time*, of a token given all previous tokens results in a sequence that follows the *joint* probability distribution (probability of co-occurrence of all tokens). This is *incredibly* powerful at scale.

If the sampling occurs from a finite vocabulary of size $K$ and the unknown tokens are represented with $\emptyset$, a sequence of length $t < T$ can be represented as $(x_1, ..., x_t, \emptyset, ..., \emptyset) \in X^T$. Although this chapter keeps $T$ fixed, it can be variable in practice.

Then, a model that takes in this $T$-sized sequence sampled from the $K$-sized vocabulary with $t$ known tokens and $T - t$ unknown $\emptyset$'s can output, at each step, a conditional probability distribution of the next token given all previous tokens across the vocabulary. The sampled token becomes part of the original sequence, and the model then takes in a sequence with $t + 1$ known tokens and $T - t - 1$ unknown $\emptyset$'s to predict the next token similarly. Each individual prediction is based on the respective conditional probability distribution; the entire sequence follows the joint probability distribution.

Such a model can be trained by setting each prediction as a classification exercise (with softmax used to generate each conditional probability distribution). The loss minimized is typically the cross entropy aggregated across *all* training pairs that represent, together, the entire sequence prediction. This can also be aggregated across training sequences, as in the case of batch gradient descent.

As seen in the previous chapter on losses, the value that is optimized is usually not the same as the value being monitored. The value being monitored here is not the cross entropy but the *perplexity* of the model, defined as the exponential of the cross entropy. This is nuanced. The conversion is simple; the intuition is nuanced.

For a given time step,

$$
ce = -\log(p)
$$

Then,

$$
e^{-\log(p)} = \frac{1}{e^{\log(p)}} = \frac{1}{p} = R
$$

where $R$ is a real number and $R > 1$ for $p < 1$. Therefore,

$$
p = \frac{1}{R}
$$

Now assume a *uniform* distribution over $R$ possibilities. All possibilities are assigned the equal probability $1/R$.

Then,

$$
ce = -\log(1/R)
$$

and therefore,

$$
e^{-\log(1/R)} = \frac{1}{e^{\log(1/R)}} = \frac{1}{1/R} = R
$$

Perplexity therefore gives cross entropy an intuitive interpretation: it represents the number of equally likely choices in a *hypothetical uniform distribution* that would have the same cross entropy as the model's actual probability distribution.

For example, suppose a model predicts the next token with probabilities 70%, 20%, and 10% for three possible tokens. This is not a uniform distribution, but it has a measurable amount of uncertainty. Perplexity converts that uncertainty into an equivalent uniform distribution: if the model's cross entropy is the same as that of a uniform distribution over roughly 2 choices, its perplexity is roughly 2. The model is *perplexed enough* to have uncertainty equivalent to choosing between two roughly equally likely possibilities for the next token.

At the sequence level, the same interpretation applies to the average cross entropy across all next-token predictions: perplexity represents the effective number of equally likely choices *per prediction* that would produce the same average uncertainty as the model's actual sequence of predictions.

## Causal models

The iterative process to generate the sequence is computationally very expensive. All computations completed for previous time steps are repeated for each successive time step. For a very large $T$, this can become prohibitively expensive.

The strategy is to embed the causal dependency in the *architecture* rather than enforcing it through sequential computation. The architecture is designed to predict the next-token logits at *all positions* together, but with a design such that the prediction for any token $x_t$ depends only on tokens, $x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}$. *One* way to accomplish this is by implementing a causal mask within the Transformer architecture, described later in the book.

One point of confusion possible here is to read the text and infer that the former approach is not causal while the latter is. Both approaches are causal. The former is sequential; the latter is not. The idea is that the dependency *remains* causal, but the computation doesn’t have to be sequential.

## Tokenizer

A *token* is one unit of vocabulary that the model can operate on. It does *not* need to be, although *may* be, a word. Tokens make up the vocabulary. They are what the model trains on, and they are what the model predicts to generate sequences.

Given the setup described in this chapter, it is apparent that *a model cannot predict a token outside of the vocabulary it trained on*. *But* it can generate words or phrases it has never seen.

There are many ways to tokenize. The way described in this chapter is Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), which seeks to strike a balance between vocabulary size and sequence length. One way to tokenize would be to split the corpus into the most granular fragments (e.g. letters or special characters). This results in a tiny vocabulary size (since most characters repeat) but *very* long sequence lengths (since each generated token is an individual character). Another way to tokenize would be to split the corpus into standalone words or word-equivalents. This results in a massive vocabulary size but short sequence lengths. And since the model can only generate *tokens* from its fixed vocabulary, a word-level tokenizer *cannot* represent words that are absent from that vocabulary.

BPE serves as middle ground. It *starts* with the most granular characters, merges adjacent characters, and repeatedly selects the most frequent adjacent pair across the corpus. The merged fragments become part of the vocabulary, and the process continues successively. The stopping criterion is a target vocabulary size. The book *does not* go into any further detail on BPE, and these notes stop here as well.
