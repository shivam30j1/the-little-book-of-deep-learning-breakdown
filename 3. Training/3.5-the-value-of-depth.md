# 3.5 The value of depth

Some value of deep learning is quite literally in the term “deep.” Models boast successive layers that _connect_ to each other, are extremely modular (not just in their size but across basically all design choices such as choice of activation, structure, operations, etc.), and can be arbitrarily extended. This results in powerful _compounding_ of “understanding,” where a well-suited deep model can intricately map the input signal to output.

Furthermore, training with gradient descent results in _co-adaption_ of these mappings. Even though updates to parameters are gradual, local, and _individual_, the compounded structure of a deep model and the mechanics of the chain rule (and perhaps other nuances) cause parameters to influence each other’s updates. Over the very gradual process of many iterations, a well-suited deep model can essentially assemble an _army of individual soldiers that work together towards the common goal of accurate prediction/inference (proxied by loss minimization)_. They develop their own internal roles, command structure, _firing_ protocol, and even _ranks_ based on relative importance. This is incredible.

The book illustrates this with a 2-dimensional input signal that is passed through 8 layers, each applying tanh as an activation function. What is more important here than the setup itself is that the data is shown to gradually re-orient itself, _successively_ as it moves forward in the network, towards a _clean_ separation that is optimal for a classification decision boundary. Since it is successive, the separation is shown to become _cleaner_ in each successive layer and become fully separable _before_ the final classification output layer. The process is automatic, gradual, and dictated by the constraints imposed by the design choices and the overall model architecture.

The illustration is on only a 2-dimensional signal. Models in practice operate on data in a much larger dimension space, which means they take advantage of many more _degrees of freedom_, or attributes they can freely optimize.

Empirical understanding suggests that state-of-the-art performance on higher dimensional signals requires particularly deep models with many layers. This is intuitive, and underscores how the modular nature of deep learning is particularly suited for high-dimensional, complex, massive data sets. It can add parameters, add layers, change activation functions, change the loss function, change the types of layers, change the connection pattern within and across layers, and much more. Inductive biases at every level of granularity in the model can be engineered to suit the input signal. However, the compounded nature of the models means that depth increases the network’s _capacity_ for understanding and transformation.

Theoretical results mentioned in the book are even more interesting. They show that, _for a fixed computational budget or number of parameters_, increasing depth leads to greater complexity of the mapping. This means that the same computational budget or number of parameters result in more complex mappings across a higher number of layers. As likely a gross simplification, _a deep model with 16 parameters potentially maps the input more intricately across 4 layers of 4 parameters each than with 2 layers of 8 parameters each_. One reason could be that deeper layers force more _specialization_, even for the same number of parameters. But the crux here remains that the ability to increase the depth of the model and consequently the number of _intermediate_ operations between input and output is one of the most powerful advantages of deep learning.
