# 3.7 The benefits of scale

An accumulation of empirical evidence suggests that performance of deep models, as judged by the decrease in loss, follows interesting scaling laws w. r. t. the amount of data, model size (parameters), and/or compute. In essence, scaling either of these levers increases model performance _predictably_ but in a way that is _brutally costly_.

Compute can be scaled independently of the deep architecture. Deep models can also be scaled up arbitrarily in size. Scaling the amount of data (tokens) the model can be trained on is more feasible due to stochastic gradient descent (SGD). Since the gradient on a partial batch of data is an unbiased estimate, under conditions, of the gradient on the full batch of data, SGD allows the model to operate on datasets of a size that is _significantly larger_ than the computing device’s memory.

Figure 3.6 in the chapter plots test loss against compute, dataset size, and number of parameters respectively in individual graphs [Kaplan et al., 2020]. Each graph shows the loss decreasing with an increase in the respective lever. For dataset size, as an example, the loss L is established as follows:

$$
L = \left(\frac{D}{5.4 \times 10^{13}}\right)^{-0.095}
$$

Then,

$$
L = \left(\frac{5.4 \times 10^{13}}{D}\right)^{0.095}
$$

As dataset size increases, the loss _decreases_, but by a _very_ small factor. This means that _massive_ increases in scale cause a _much smaller_ relative decline in loss, but a _predictable decline_ nonetheless as dictated by the power law. Similar equations for compute and model size mean that scaling laws are brutal, and substantial, tail-end optimizations of the loss require massive scaling. Something _truly amazing_ then happens at enormous scale as sophisticated capabilities of modern, state-of-the-art large models emerge.

Training these large models at frontier scale then requires a scale of compute, model size, and tokens most available to the most sophisticated, deeply capitalized, tech forward organizations or AI labs. Datasets used to train are typically gigantic and used with minimal curation, if any. These datasets can span across modalities such as text, images, and videos. Modern large models have hundreds of billions of parameters and are backed by massive compute and massive data.
