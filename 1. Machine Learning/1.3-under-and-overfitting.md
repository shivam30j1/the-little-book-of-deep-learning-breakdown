#1.3 Underfitting and overfitting

Both concepts are explained very briefly in the book, except for the key idea of inductive bias.

Consider a machine learning model being trained on a dataset i. A model that lacks the expressive capacity to represent the underlying structure of the data will perform poorly during training. No amount of training data will mitigate the issue since the deficiency lies in the inherent structure of the model, which is not suited to the structure of the training data. The model performs poorly on the training data and any unseen samples. This is underfitting.

In contrast, a model with _too much_ expressive capacity for i will not only learn the underlying structure of the training data but also assign predictive power to its noise. The model performs excellently on the training data since it maps it almost perfectly. It _does not_ perform well on unseen samples. This is overfitting. Overfitting is mitigated by, among other things, increasing the size of i or introducing regularization that discourages unnecessarily complex solutions.

Much of machine learning is about balancing the expressive power of the model and the amount/quality of training data. The goal is to develop a model that is not _so expressive_ that it captures noise but _expressive enough_ that it captures the structure of the training data. This is typically achieved by choosing or developing architectures with the appropriate inductive bias.

This perspective is valid for classical machine learning models as well as decently sized deep models. However, it gets interesting with very large deep models that have a huge number of parameters and extreme capacity and _still_ perform well on prediction. There’s more discussion on this later in the book, but my guess for one reason is: the author talks about how a lot of conventional wisdom breaks down at scale, both of data and of the model. It could be that a sufficiently expressive model, given enough data and appropriate training, _can learn what patterns consistently help make accurate predictions and what patterns are just noise_. With enough capacity, the model is no longer forced to explain the data using every apparent correlation it finds and can instead represent the underlying predictive structure more faithfully.

Inductive bias is a _foundational_ concept. As one goes through the book, they realize that inductive bias is a recurring theme behind many of the design choices in deep learning. Formally, inductive biases are the set of assumptions that a machine learning model (or a component thereof) makes about the data it is trying to learn from. A dataset used for training can, in theory, be generated from any one of infinitely many data-generating processes. The training samples alone do not uniquely reveal which process generated them.

A model has useful inductive bias for a problem when its structure is suited to capture the underlying structure of the data. This is what allows the model to generalize beyond the training samples to unseen examples from the same data-generating process. The reason this is necessary is that generalization itself requires an inductive leap: the model must infer patterns from finite observations and assume that those patterns will continue to hold beyond the data it has seen. Without some form of inductive bias, this leap is impossible, because _infinitely many_ functions can explain the same training data while making completely different predictions on unseen samples.

This is crucial to understand _before_ going through the rest of the book, specifically the various layers and architectures. Critical inductive biases emerge while studying these concepts. Since deep learning models are modular, successive, and compounded, inductive biases emerge at _all levels_ of the model, from the overall architecture to individual components (e.g. layers). This could be another reason why deep learning is so powerful: it allows for _modularizing_ inductive biases, where different components of the architecture possess inductive biases for different structures of incoming signals.
